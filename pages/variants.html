Scorpion: three bodies, three jobs

The same robot inside every one: head v2, the Pi with the Hailo, the PCA9685, both packs, the ESCs and the power parts. Each body is pushed toward one goal, rendered and measured the same way, against the redesign you liked (bottom). A brainstorm: nothing here is fit-checked yet.

Low · sleek · quick to print

Scout

Indoor runs, fitting under furniture, and the fastest first print.

What changes

  • One flat deck at the height of the head's well: only the ball sticks up.
  • No raised hull: the Pi, the PCA9685 and the power parts move into the August brief's low tail bay, which ends in an antenna stub.
  • Small, light tracks: 22 mm sprockets, 5 road wheels, skirts over the top run.
  • Claws kept, lowered, for lights and range sensors.

Gains

  • Centre of mass 41 mm off the ground, against 78: it tips sideways only past 71°.
  • 143 mm tall, against 281 with the arm.
  • 647 g of plastic, about 26 h: 28% less than the baseline.

Costs

  • Ground clearance 19 mm, approach 12°, departure 11°: the low tracks set those. Floors and carpets only.
  • Least room inside (1.9 L) and nowhere for an arm.
  • Longest (386 mm), because the tail bay hangs out the back.

Terrain · survival

Crawler

Outdoors, kerbs and steps, and coming back in one piece.

What changes

  • Big trapezoid tracks: 30 mm drive sprocket, raised front idler, 64 wide, 5 road wheels on arms.
  • Front flippers on the idler axle, pincer-tipped: they swing down to climb a step or flip the robot back over (like iRobot's PackBot).
  • Sloped armour, a skid plate under the belly, hooded louvres.
  • A roll hoop behind the head guards the ball if it rolls over, and doubles as the carry handle.

Gains

  • Ground clearance 48 mm, approach 31° (flippers raised), departure 36°: the best of the four.
  • The ball survives a roll-over.
  • Same room inside as the baseline (2.8 L).

Costs

  • Widest by far (362 mm across the flippers) and heaviest (3336 g).
  • Most plastic: 1043 g, about 42 h.
  • Two more drives for the flippers, each a standard servo or a geared motor.

The arm · payload

Workhorse

Picking things up, carrying them, working around the robot.

What changes

  • The long two-joint arm (option B, 110 + 290) moves to mid-ship, over the centre of mass.
  • It reaches the ground in front, beside and behind the robot. Here it reaches in front of the left track, passing beside the head.
  • A cargo bed with tie-down slots and rails behind the arm; the packs sit aft as counterweight.
  • Brain access moves under the arm's pedestal and to side panels.

Gains

  • The arm reaches the ground in front of the robot; the baseline's rear-mounted arm only reaches over it.
  • Lower centre of mass than the baseline (74 vs 78 mm), even reaching out with 100 g, and steadier backward (43° vs 40°).
  • Same hull as the baseline: almost no new design.

Costs

  • The arm swings over the head: software has to keep the forearm high near the ball, the same kind of rule as the turret's tilt/pan rule.
  • A 400 mm arm wobbles at the tip; the pushrod pins need bearings.
  • No tail: the arm is the scorpion part.

The baseline

The hull redesign with the ideal tracks and the two-joint arm on the rear deck. Its arm can reach over the head but not down in front; everything else is the middle of the pack.

If I had to pick

The Workhorse, with the Crawler's roll hoop. It's the baseline hull with the arm where it can actually work, and it comes out steadier, not worse. The ball is the most expensive thing on the robot to break; the hoop costs one bent tube.

Steal from the Scout for the first print: its flat deck and low tail bay are the cheapest way to get the electronics, the packs and the head into one body and driving.

The Crawler's flippers are the only option that climbs a stair. Worth it only if stairs or self-righting become goals.
